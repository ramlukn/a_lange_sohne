Research

Harvard Medical School

Undergraduate Researcher, Blavatnik Institute

Cambridge, MA · HybridJuly 2024 — Present

I work with Dr. Kee B. Park on passive, phone-based detection of early neurological impairment. I train multimodal deep-learning classifiers on audio and image samples to pick up neuromotor and speech biomarkers.

  • The sensors are the ones already in a phone: microphone, camera, accelerometer, gyroscope. Nobody sits for a test and nothing is instrumented. So far I have trained on the audio and the image.
  • The signal is the motor and speech change that runs ahead of diagnosis — tremor, bradykinesia and postural instability in movement; dysarthria and falling verbal fluency in speech. In the image it is facial palsy. The conditions are Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and ischemic stroke.
  • Each sample carries one of two labels, so the model returns a score and a threshold turns it into a call: who to look at sooner, not what they have. Sooner is the whole point. Earlier intervention in the degenerative conditions, shorter time to treatment in the acute one.
  • Park's field is global neurosurgery, and that sets the bar for the models: they have to hold across countries and health systems, not only inside the population they were trained on. The long-run goal is early detection that works as well in a low-resource setting as in a high one.
  • The argument for the approach, co-authored with Park: AANS Neurosurgeon, November 2024.

Johns Hopkins School of Medicine

Undergraduate Researcher, Laboratory of Computational Intensive Care Medicine

Baltimore, MD · On-siteNovember 2025 — Present

Under Dr. Robert David Stevens, I develop and evaluate machine-learning models that stratify the risk of complications after an elective craniotomy. The models are developed on MIMIC-IV and then evaluated on a Johns Hopkins PMAP cohort: a different hospital and a different population.

  • The unit is one patient and one operation. What comes out is a ranking, not a verdict — who is likely to develop complications and who is not, rather than which complication or when.
  • That ranking is for one thing: how closely a patient is watched once they leave the operating room.
  • MIMIC-IV is a critical-care database from a single hospital, released through PhysioNet: admissions, vital signs, laboratory results, medications and procedures, all of it recorded in the course of care and de-identified before release. The Hopkins cohort is the same kind of record, kept at a different institution.
  • One hospital's data can only say how well a model fits that hospital. What the second cohort asks is whether the ranking still means anything when the patients, the surgeons and the recording habits change.
  • Stevens is a neurocritical care physician, and the lab he founded is an engineering-and-medicine group that builds AI systems for patients in intensive care. That is the field the question is asked in.
